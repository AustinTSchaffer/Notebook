> MacKenzie, I.S. (2013). Chapter 4: Scientific Foundations. _Human-Computer Interaction: An Empirical Research Perspective_. (pp. 121-152). Waltham, MA: Elsevier.

## What is research?
1. Careful or diligent search
2. collecting information about a particular subject
3. investigation or experimentation aimed at the discovery and interpretation of facts and revision of accepted theories or laws in light of new facts

We care about the 3rd definition

- "experimentation" aka a "user study"
- "empirical research"
- "facts", the building blocks of evidence
- Nothing is "proven" in the context of HCI research
- "theories"
	- synonymous with "hypothesis"
	- through experimentation, advanced beyond argument and investigation
- "laws"
	- more specific
	- more constraining
	- more formal
	- more binding
	- "invariable under given conditions"
- "discovery"
- "interpretation"
- "revision"

### Research must be published
- edict "publish or perish"
- research cannot achieve its critical purpose unless it is published
	- extend, refine, revise
	- add to the existing body of knowledge in a field
- bears a high standard of scrutiny
- requires publication in peer-reviewed journals or conference proceedings
- review is by peers
	- peers = other researchers doing similar work
	- results are novel and useful?
	- evidence supports the conclusions?
	- contribution to the field?
	- methodology meet the expected standards for research?
- "archived"
	- work is added to the collection of related work
	- archived work is accessible to other researchers throughout the world
	- "archived" research is the "existing body of knowledge"

### Citations, references, impact

- citations connect research papers to other research papers
- body of research takes its shape as a DAG
- full bibliographic entries in the references section at the end of a research paper
- support intellectual honesty
- acknowledges that the paper continue, extend, or refine earlier research
- backs up questionable assertions
- compare current results to earlier results

### Research vs engineering vs design
- engineers and designers create products that strive to bring together the best in form and function
	- form = design emphasis
	- function = engineering emphasis
- research questions are generally smaller in scope
- research tends to be incremental, not monumental
- engineers and designers work with prototypes
	- used to assess alternatives at a relatively late stage (part of product development)
	- early mock-up of an idea
	- prototypes are also useful for research

- research precedes engineering and design
- the march forward for research is at a slower pace
	- no shackles
	- no deadlines (unless you don't want someone publishing your idea first)

## What is empirical research?
- originating in or based on observation or experience
- relying on experience or observation alone, often without due regard for system and theory
- capable of being verified or disproved by observation or experiment

## Research methods

### Observational method
- observation is the starting point
- qualitative rather than quantitative
- achieve relevance, while sacrificing precision
- "why" or "how"

### Experimental method
- aka the "scientific method"
- knowledge is acquired through controlled expieriments condicted in laboratory settings
	- gathering new knowledge
	- studying existing knowledge
		- verifying
		- refuting
		- correcting
		- integrating
		- extending
- prioritizes precision over relevance
- requires at least two variables
	- manipulated variable
		- independent variable (IV)
		- factor
	- response variable
		- dependent variable (DV)

### Correlational method
- looking for relationships between variables
- characterized by quantification
- data is collected, then relationships are examined
- magnitude of variables must be ascertained (age, income, etc)
- provides balance between relevance and precision
- data obtained using correlational methods are circumstantial, not causal. proving causation is harder than finding correlation.

## Research questions
Questions can pertain to the relationship between 2 variables. In HCI, one is a circumstance or condition that is manipulated. The other is an observed and measured behavioral response.

- Variability affects the confidence with which we can answer research questions
- We gauge confidence using statistics
- Initial thoughts are fluid and informal
	- viable?
	- good or better?
	- Strengths and weaknesses?
	- which alternative is best?

## Internal and external validity
![[Pasted image 20250603214313.png]]

- internal validity = the extent to which an effect observed is due to the test conditions
- external validity = the extent to which experimental results are generalizable to other people in other situations

The tension between IntV and ExtV is is similar to what happens in ML with respect to high training accuracy (IntV) and high testing accuracy (ExtV)

## Relationships: circumstantial and causal
- cause-and-effect relationship = causal relationship
- Finding a relationship does not necessarily mean a causal relationship exists. Many relationships are circumstantial.
- It's easy to find circumstantial relationships, and they can often lead to prompts for further research.
- Causal relationships emerge from controlled experiments

> Cause and effect conclusions are not possible in certain types of controlled experiments. If the variable manipulated is a naturally occurring attribute of participants, then cause and effect conclusions are unreliable. \[...\] These attributes are legitimate independent variables, but they cannot be manipulated, which is to say, they cannot be assigned to participants. In such cases, a cause and effect conclusion is not valid because is not possible to avoid confounding variables.

### Tips for finding a topic
- Think small.
	- graduate students are often saddled with big problems because a supervisor's funding source requires it
	- narrow the problem
	- the smaller the better
	- forget about finding a big idea
- replicate
	- replicate an existing experiment
	- replicating an experiment requires a lot of work
	- precisely determining what was done, then implementing it, testing it, debugging it, doing an experiment, this empowers the researcher with a deep understanding, much deeper than simply reading the paper.
	- Often a new idea or twist emerges during the process of replication.
	- Avoid a requirement for novelty.
	- This may also lead to showing the prior art is not replicable.
- know the literature
	- reviewing papers on a topic of interest is an excellent way to develop ideas for research projects
	- aggressively study and analyze all published research on a topic
	- if there's too many publications, narrow the topic
	- Use the tools
		- Google Scholar
		- ACM Digital Library
	- store the papers and organize them
	- take notes
	- tabulate features from the papers in a spreadsheet
	- from chaos, find order
- think inside the box
	- you are already inside the box
	- challenge your own experiences
