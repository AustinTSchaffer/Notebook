# 3.3.R1 - Survey Research in HCI
> Müller, H., Sedley, A., & Ferrall-Nunge, E. (2014). Survey research in HCI. In J. Olson & W. Kellogg (Eds.) _Ways of Knowing in HCI_ (pp. 229-266). New York: Springer.

- a survey is a method of gathering information
- asking questions to a subset of people
- the results can ideally be generalized to the wider target population
- collect people's attitudes and perceptions
- understand people's intents and motivations for using an application
- capture awareness
- surveys do not allow for observation of the respondents' context or follow-up questions

## History, Intellectual Tradition, Evolution
- User happiness is fundamental to Google's HEART framework for user-centric measurement of Web applications
## What questions the method can answer
- insights into users
	- attitudes
	- experiences
	- intents
	- demographics
	- psychographic characteristics
- surveys are not the most appropriate method for many other HCI research goals. Other options:
	- ethnographic interfviews
	- log data analysis
	- card sorts
	- usability studies
- When they're appropriate
	- attitudes/perceptions of a population
	- statistically reliable metrics are desired
	- tie self-reported attitudes to actual behavior
	- capture intent of a population
	- task success. surveys can reliably quantify levels of success
	- user experience feedback
	- user characteristics
	- interactions with technology
	- awareness
	- comparisons
- When to avoid surveys. If you're looking to capture X, probably don't bother with a survey:
	- precise behavior
	- underlying motivations
	- Usability evaluations

## Research goals and constructs
- when the survey-appropriate research goals have been identified, they should be matched to constructs (unidimensional attributes that cannot be directly observed)
- thorough factor analysis may help the researcher ensure the construct's validity
- cognitive pretesting can be used to determine whether respondents are interpreting the constructs as intended by the researcher
- an excess of "nice-to-know" questions increases survey length, increasing the likelihood that respondents will not complete the questionnaire
## Population and Sampling
- population
	- the set of individuals that meet certain criteria
	- a survey of everyone is a "census"
- sampling frame
	- the set of people who the researcher is able to contact for the survey
- sample
	- the people from the sampling frame who are invited to take the survey
- respondents
	- the people who actually answer the questions

### Random Sampling vs Non-Probability Sampling
- random sampling
	- considered the gold-standard because every person in the sampling frame has a non-zero chance of being chosen for the sample.
	- reduces/minimizes sampling/selection bias
- non-probability sampling
	- volunteer
	- opt-in
	- unrestricted self-selected surveys
	- snowball recruiting
	- convenience sampling
	- high sampling bias
	- reduce representativeness

### Types of Survey Questions
- open ended
- nominal
- ordinal
- multiple choice is just grouping binary nominal data.
- ranking
- rating

### Questionnaire Biases
- Satisficing = when respondents use a suboptimal amount of cognitive effort to answer questions
- Acquiescence Bias = Respondents are more likely to concur with agree/disagree/yes/no/true/false questions
- Social Desirability = "Does this interface suck?" No it's great! You did a great job. Don't let me tell you otherwise.
- Response Order Bias = tendency to select items toward the beginning (primacy effect) or the end (recency effect)
- Question Order Bias

### Avoid these questions
- broad questions (lack of focus)
- leading questions (be impartial)
- double-barreled questions (ask one thing at a time)
- recall questions (human memory is bad. You're not asking questions to a database, you're making one.)
- Prediction questions

## Leveraging Established Questionnaires
